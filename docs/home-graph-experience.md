# Home, As Experienced

> A phenomenological description of home — what a person sees, feels, and does,
> written deliberately WITHOUT implementation vocabulary. No file names, no
> library names, no architecture. The point: this document is the design
> material. Iterate on *it* — rewrite what the experience *should* be — and only
> then ask what has to change underneath. Describing the experience separately
> from the mechanism is how we escape status-quo bias: the current behaviour is
> recorded faithfully here, including its accidents, so that every sentence can
> be challenged on experiential grounds alone.
>
> Two registers are used throughout. **IS** — what happens today. **ACCIDENT?**
> — a property that may exist only because of how it was built, flagged so a
> redesign knows it is negotiable.
>
> The scope has grown since the first edition. This is no longer a description
> of a graph view; it is a description of a *place* with two grounds — a valley
> in daylight and a sky at night — the threshold between them, and the sheets of
> paper you read on both sides.

---

## 0. The model: two grounds, one sky

Home is one place seen in two postures.

**The valley** — daylight, painted mountains, a cream sheet of ground content
you can scroll and read like a page. This is the trailhead: where you land,
where documents open, where a visitor without a key stands too.

**The sky** — night, your substrate as your own night sky, and you *inside* it:
on the ground under a dome of stars, not above a map of them. Direction on the
sphere is meaning; brightness and size are attention; nothing is culled from
the shell, only dimmed.

They are not two apps and not two pages. The sky is already there at the
trailhead — a live band of night above the painted mountains — and the passage
between the two is a single continuous gesture in either direction (§2, §9).
Day and night are the same territory at different depths of engagement.

**In the sky, you never move. The sky does.** There is no camera to fly, no
orbit, no travelling toward things. A single continuous zoom bends *the sky
itself*. Fully curled, it wraps around you — the planetarium. Unroll it and it
peels open around the point you are facing until it lies flat — a planisphere —
and further still it re-curls the other way into a **globe held at arm's
length**. Dome, chart, globe are three points on one motion, and you can stop
anywhere along it. One drag turns it with the same hand-feel everywhere on the
curl.

- **IS**: one fixed viewpoint; zoom is a continuous curl; drag rotates the
  shell; the trailhead and the sky are one continuous place.
- **ACCIDENT?**: that the far end of the curl is a *held globe* is one chosen
  resolution of "what is the most zoomed-out thing."

## 1. The threshold

You arrive in the valley. Painted mountains, a dusk band of real sky above them
— and that band is *live*: your actual stars, already turning, already filling
in. The title of whatever the ground is showing sits over the treeline; below
the horizon, the ground content lies on a cream sheet with a soft rounded lip,
like paper resting on the landscape.

The sky band is touchable from the porch. **Drag it and it spins** — freely,
without stealing the page's scroll, without tipping you into the night. **Pinch
and it magnifies** — but only as a telescope under the dome; from the porch you
cannot unfurl the sky into the chart or the globe. Tap a star and the ground
below turns to that fact. The preview is a real window, not a screenshot — it
just refuses to swallow you uninvited.

Two handles say what pulls where. The cream sheet's lip carries a **grip** — a
small pill, the same grip the night-side instrument wears — and pulling it
*down* is the door into the sky. The hero text says so in words the first time;
after that the pill is enough.

- **IS**: the graph preview is a strip of true sky (about two-thirds the height
  of the painted hero) — the stars live in the sky band above the mountains,
  not behind them. Spin and tap-select are free; zoom is clamped to the
  planetarium; the enter gesture lives only on the grip (and, with a mouse, an
  over-scroll at the top of the page).
- **ACCIDENT?**: the exact seam where painting ends and live sky begins is an
  art-direction choice; so is hiding the door-gesture from the sky itself
  (you cannot fall into the night by touching the stars — only by the grip).

## 2. Walking in

Entering is not a cut. It is a gesture you *feel the whole way*.

You pull the grip down and the world answers in proportion: the sky band grows
toward full height exactly as fast as your finger travels; the painted valley
dims toward dusk; the air deepens; the cream sheet slides down and slightly
faster than the mountains behind it, the way near things do. A quiet line of
text keeps score — *keep pulling* — until, past a definite weight, it changes
to *release to enter*. Let go early and everything springs back to daylight,
no harm done. Let go past the commit and the day dissolves — the painting and
the dusk wash fade over a breath, and you are standing under the whole sky,
which was already full-height under your finger before you released. Nothing
snaps. The night does not *load*; it was there all along.

- **IS**: the transition is gesture-tracked (world follows the finger 1:1),
  with a fixed commit weight, an elastic cancel, and a dissolve only after the
  commit. The same door works by wheel at the top of the page.
- **ACCIDENT?**: the commit distance and the dissolve duration are tuned
  numbers, not laws. That the *text* changes at the commit point (rather than
  some purely visual signal) is a choice.

## 3. Arrival in the sky

The first band of stars is already there — the brightest, most-alive things
first, the field filling inward from importance. A quiet counter tells you how
much of your world is charted so far — "800 of 6,500" — and then the map keeps
charting *itself*, a couple hundred stars at a time, until your whole substrate
is present. One control pauses the in-fill.

You always land *looking at something*: the sky turns to bring your single
most-alive thing to face you. Unless you arrived by a **link** — the address
quietly records which star is selected, how curled the sky is, and any query
you have running, so a shared link reopens exactly that view.

The field does **not** drift on its own. A place you inhabit holds still; the
sphere waits for your hand.

A visitor without a key stands under the *same* sky — the public face of the
substrate, tuned identically, spinning and reading with the same hands, minus
only the acts that change things.

- **IS**: importance-first arrival, faced at the top star; visible self-charting;
  URL-restorable view; stillness at rest; one tuned sky for owner and visitor
  alike.
- **ACCIDENT?**: the amount charted before the first pause is a budget, not an
  intention.

## 4. What the picture says before you touch it

Everything visible encodes something, though none of it is explained on screen:

- **Position is meaning.** Neighbourhoods are topics; the shape of the field is
  the shape of what you think about.
- **Light is attention.** Brightness and size track how alive a thing is; the
  field is a picture of your attention, not just your content.
- **Colour is kind.** Each type of thing carries its own hue. No legend.
- **Names are earned, from one budget.** A single mind-sized focus band spent
  across three voices — landmarks (steady territory names), focus (the ignited
  selection), suggestions (faint candidates near your gaze) — that never
  compete on one size gradient.
- **Threads are relations.** Fine lines connect stars; at rest a near-subliminal
  webbing, under focus an accent-lit fan with a travelling current. When a star
  is selected, the relations themselves take **words**: each edge of the fan
  carries its verb — *grounds →*, *← refines*, *inDoc →* — a small tappable
  word riding the line. The word sits at the true middle of however much of the
  line you can see, at any zoom, and slides along its own line to free space
  rather than pile onto a neighbour. Repeated verbs repeat — three memberships
  read as three words — because the fan is a list of doors, and every door is
  labelled. Tapping a verb walks through it to the far star.

- **IS**: no legend, no axes; comprehension by acquaintance; relation words are
  selection-only, capped, placed on the visible span, never dropped for
  crowding — they move instead.
- **ACCIDENT?**: the cap on how many relation words show at once is a tuned
  number. Whether repeated verbs should ever coalesce is a live question —
  today they never do, by decision.

## 5. Moving through it: turn, curl, and the glass instruments

**Turning.** Drag, and the stars wheel past; fling, and the sky coasts to rest
like a spun globe. The horizon stays level no matter what.

**Curling.** Wheel or pinch runs the one zoom axis of §0 — telescope first,
then the unfurl, no seam, no mode.

**The air.** Under the dome the sky deepens from a warm horizon glow to a dark
zenith, and the gradient turns with the *world*, not your eye — you know which
way is up before you find a landmark. It thins away as you unfurl; a chart has
no airglow.

**The instruments.** In the top corner floats a small strip of frosted glass —
the same glass as the field computer below (§6) — holding three quiet controls:
a **ring gauge** whose golden sweep shows how much of the field is lit (tap it
to swing between the vital few and everything; press and drag to scrub anywhere
between), a **vantage toggle** that glides you between standing under the dome
and holding the globe, and the **charting counter**. Instruments, not chrome:
they sit over the sky like a watch face, not a toolbar.

- **IS**: navigation is turn + curl from one fixed viewpoint; the depth dial is
  a scrubbable icon; vantage is one tap; everything else is gesture.
- **ACCIDENT?**: the instrument strip's corner placement, and which controls
  earned icons versus which live in the field computer, are inherited layouts.

## 6. Touching one thing: the field computer

You tap a star. The map is forgiving about aim; wobbly touches count.

Selection happens in **two beats**: first tap, it lights in place — ring,
ignited neighbourhood, labelled fan — and the sky holds still. Second tap on
the same star, the sky turns to face it. Motion only on request.

What opens below is the **field computer**: one sheet of frosted glass resting
at the bottom of the sky, the stars faintly visible through it. It reads as a
single instrument, not a stack of panels. On it:

- The fact's **name first** — full reading size, wrapping, styled if the name
  itself carries emphasis — with its kind and vitals under it. Nothing shouts
  over the title; the verbs that can act on it are quiet chips below.
- Its **connections as chips**, each carrying its verb and direction; tapping
  one drills the selection to that star.
- A **search line** — the same line that answers questions (§7) — always one
  glance away at the sheet's foot.

The sheet is worked by **one grip and a ladder of drags**. Pull up: the fact's
body unfolds in place; pull up again: the console opens over it. Pull down:
these close in reverse — body, then console — and one more pull *minimises* the
whole instrument to a slim strip: just the fact's name and a ×, the sky nearly
bare, the selection still held and still looked-at. Tap the strip and the
computer returns. The ladder's very top is the way home (§9).

Drilling leaves a **trail**: step through connections or search picks and a
small ‹ appears beside the title — your path back through what you were
reading, one step per drill. Turning to a different star by tapping the sky
*replaces* rather than pushes — back means "return to what I was reading," not
"undo every look."

Reading in sheets and turning the sky are **decoupled on purpose**: what you
open in the reading stack re-aims the sky behind it, but never rewrites the
valley's ground content. Only touching a star in the sky does that.

- **IS**: two-beat selection; one glass sheet with a grip-ladder
  (body → console → minimised); a drill trail with ‹; selection-sync from
  sheets is aim-only.
- **ACCIDENT?**: the ladder's rung order is a design in progress; the minimised
  strip keeping the search line (rather than vanishing entirely) is a choice
  made this week and revisitable.

## 7. Asking instead of wandering

The search line accepts language. Type a phrase and the answer comes back *on
the map*: the matching set lights up, and the sky turns to bring the whole
answer to centre. Results also exist as a list under the line for precision,
and picking one is a drill — it joins the trail like any other step.

- **IS**: query → highlight → face; results as lit territory first, list second.
- **ACCIDENT?**: wander and ask are equal citizens; a redesign could
  subordinate one.

## 8. Reading: the stack of sheets

Follow a link inside any document — a wiki-link, a member of a composite, a
"part of" chip — and the fact opens as a **sheet**: cream paper with a rounded
lip, the same paper as the valley's ground. Follow another and it stacks. This
is one drill history with a cursor, and it behaves like paper, not like windows:

**In the valley, the stack is the ground.** Opening a sheet docks it just below
the ground's own lip — the page composes itself: hero and live sky above, the
ground's tip peeking, the new sheet under it. The ground's body steps aside;
the sheet's content *is* the page now, and you read it by scrolling the page —
no box-within-a-box, no inner scrollbar. Sheets you drilled past peek as thin
lips above the current one, nesting under the ground's tip. The world above
stays live: the graph re-aims to whatever you're reading.

**Back is a pull.** Drag the sheet's header down and what you are returning to
is revealed *underneath* — the previous sheet's head, or, on the last sheet,
the valley's actual ground content, already there behind the paper you're
sliding away. Release past the weight and you're back; your scroll position in
the sheet you return to is **remembered** — every sheet keeps its own place,
and closing the whole stack returns the ground to wherever you'd left it.

**Forward waits at the bottom.** Stepping back doesn't destroy where you'd
been: the forward pile rests at the foot of the screen, next-on-top, deeper
sheets peeking beneath it. Drag it up and it *grows* — its own content rising
under your finger — until release commits the advance. Nothing reloads on any
of this; every sheet in the stack is already there, and flipping through them
is instant.

**In the sky, the stack floats.** The same history, dark-toned, resting above
the minimised field computer (which tucks itself down so the strip and search
line stay visible beneath the paper). It is a modal nowhere: no dimmed world
behind it, no tap-outside-to-lose-everything — the sky spins and the valley
scrolls while sheets are up; only ×, Esc, and the pulls dismiss.

- **IS**: one history, two dressings (docked paper in the valley, floating
  paper in the sky); page-scroll reading; per-sheet remembered scrolls;
  next-on-top forward pile that grows on drag; nothing remounts on flips;
  drag-to-ground on the last sheet commits home and shows the real ground on
  the way.
- **ACCIDENT?**: the stack caps its visible lips at three; the pile's rest
  height, the commit weights, and the dock's exact offset under the ground's
  tip are tuned numbers.

## 9. The return

Leaving the sky is the arrival played backwards, and it is *earned the same
way* — by a pull, tracked the whole way.

Pull the field computer's grip up past the top of its ladder and the world
begins to come back: the valley fades in and slides up into place, the painting
brightening from dusk toward day exactly as far as your finger has travelled;
the sky shrinks toward its preview band; the vantage glides home under the
dome so the trailhead never arrives holding a globe it can't hold; the
instrument itself rides your finger up as it dissolves. The same scorekeeping
line appears — *keep pulling*, then *release to step back*. Let go early and
the night springs back whole. Let go past the weight and you are standing in
the valley again.

What the return *means* depends on what you were holding. With a fact peeked,
pulling up is a **commit to reading it**: the valley arrives already showing
that fact full on the ground — expanding the peek and leaving the sky are one
motion. With only the console open, the pull simply walks you out. The wordmark
in the corner plays the same choreography from a tap.

- **IS**: gesture-tracked mirror of entry — same commit weight, same elastic
  cancel, painting brightening on the same curve it dimmed; peek-pull promotes
  the peeked fact to the ground; vantage returns to the dome.
- **ACCIDENT?**: entry is pull-*down* and return is pull-*up* — the vertical
  metaphor (the sky is up, the valley is down) emerged rather than being
  chosen, and could be challenged.

## 10. The field is alive

The map is not a snapshot. Every few seconds it quietly reconciles with
reality: a note captured from your phone appears in its neighbourhood; a
retired thing fades; an agent working elsewhere leaves new points of light.
Nothing announces these arrivals — they are simply *there* on the next glance,
the way a city seen from a hill has new lights at midnight.

Living with it produces a distinct feeling: the map is a *cohabited* place.
Attention you (or your agents) spend anywhere in the workspace slowly reshapes
the brightness of the field itself.

- **IS**: ambient, unannounced liveness on a ~12-second heartbeat.
- **ACCIDENT?**: the silence of arrivals — legible change (trails, pulses, a
  "since you last looked" sweep) is a choice currently made by omission.

## 11. Two weathers

The sky has two moods, switchable: **dusk** (the luminous dark field — glow,
bloom, atmosphere; a night flight over your own mind) and **paper** (the same
territory as a printed star-atlas — ink stars on warm cream, more captions, no
glow; cartographic daylight). The same territory serves two postures —
immersive orientation and studied reading — and the person chooses the
register. The valley, of course, is a third weather that was there all along.

- **ACCIDENT?**: exactly two sky-weathers. "Weather" could be a spectrum.

## 12. Feel

The micro-texture matters and is deliberate:

- **Thresholds are gestures, in both directions.** Nothing important happens on
  a tap you can't feel the weight of: entering, leaving, popping a sheet,
  advancing the pile — all are pulls with the same grammar (track the finger,
  commit past a weight, spring back on release). The two big transitions are
  exact mirrors, so the place feels reversible rather than navigated.
- **One material per world.** The night's surfaces are one sheet of frosted
  glass — the stars read through every panel, and its frost and depth are
  themselves tunable, like everything else about the sky's feel. The day's
  surfaces are one cream paper — the ground, the sheets, the lips, all the
  same stock, stacking like paper actually stacks.
- Nothing pops. Labels fade, the curl eases, arrivals fill. The one sanctioned
  snap is the selection ring.
- Biased toward calm: at rest the sky under-informs on purpose and holds
  perfectly still. Its failure mode is chosen to be "too quiet," never "too
  loud."
- Forgiveness over precision: fat-finger tolerance, drags never misread as
  taps, momentum everywhere. Built to be *petted*, on a phone, lying down.
- Depth cues are atmospheric, never diagrammed.

## 13. The honest frictions

Recorded so a redesign argues with reality, not a brochure:

1. **The far side.** "Reach the star behind this one" is still done by turning
   the globe. Occlusion is a dial now, not an accident, but not *nothing*.
2. **Bearing drifts.** The shell turns freely; landmarks re-anchor it, but
   there is no fixed north between visits.
3. **The encodings are esoteric.** Light/size/colour/position all mean things
   and none are stated. First-contact comprehension is near zero; the map
   selects for people willing to acquire it by use.
4. **Gesture literacy is the new esotericism.** The grips are discoverable
   pills, but the *ladder* — body, console, minimise, exit, all on one handle —
   and the sheet-stack's pulls are conventions you learn by trying. The words
   that coach entry ("pull the handle below") have no counterparts inside.
5. **Emptiness is unexplained.** Darkness might mean "nothing there," "not yet
   charted," or "thinned by the dial" — three truths with one appearance.
6. **The map shows, but barely *does*.** Almost every consequential act still
   happens on the sheets and the computer, not the sky. As a workplace the sky
   is nearly read-only; as a place it is where you live. Unresolved.
7. **The valley's ground and the sheets can still shear.** The decoupling rules
   (what re-aims the sky, what rewrites the ground, what the return promotes)
   are right individually but subtle in sum; the model is felt before it is
   understood.
8. **Accessibility is unaddressed.** Wholly visual-spatial and motion-rich; no
   non-visual equivalent, no reduced-motion posture, colour-meaning with no
   redundant channel. The gesture-only thresholds raise the stakes.

## 14. Prompts for the next description

Questions to answer *in experiential terms* before touching anything real:

- The valley and the sky are now one continuous place with a felt threshold.
  Should there be a *third* depth — a posture past the sky (the held globe as
  its own destination, with its own ground)?
- Is light *importance* or *neglect*? Should the sky show "what I'm ignoring"
  as vividly as "what I'm touching"?
- Should selection *do* more — is the sky a viewer with instruments, or a
  workbench where linking, retiring, and annotating are direct manipulations?
- The sheets remember scroll, order, and trail. Should they remember across
  *sessions* — is the stack a transient reading posture or a persistent desk?
- Should change be legible? What does "what happened here since yesterday" look
  like as weather rather than as a list?
- Who is the map for on a shared substrate — what does *someone else's*
  attention look like in your sky, and what should a visitor's presence feel
  like in yours?
