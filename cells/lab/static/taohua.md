# Taohua · 桃花

A climb up a stone stair through peach blossom, to the mountain temples, on forever. After a
print: a hillside of peach trees in flower, black rocks along a zigzag of steps, a temple of teal
tiles and red columns at the top, a walker in a straw hat with a staff, and down its side Bai
Juyi's couplet on the temple at Dalin:

> 人间四月芳菲尽，山寺桃花始盛开。
> *In the world below, April's flowers are all gone; at the mountain temple, the peach blossom is just opening.*

Tap the path and the walker goes there, up or back down; press on the path and slide along it, and
they follow your finger. Only path you can see answers a tap: a tap on blossom, a rock, a temple, or
anywhere off the path does nothing, so the walker never goes where you can't see them arrive. (↑, W
walk on up; ↓, S back down.) Drag anywhere off the path to look about a little; let go, and the view
comes back. The steps you've climbed are counted at the top. At each temple you pass,
its name comes up at the foot of the print over 桃花, and the next couplet down the side: Bai
Juyi's poem through, then Cui Hu's 人面桃花, Su Shi, Li Bai, and Tao Yuanming's peach blossom
spring.

## The hill

The path is made as you climb, piece by piece, from the seed and each piece's number, so the same
seed is the same hillside, step for step (`?seed=…`). Most pieces are flights of five to sixteen
steps, each turned a little or a lot to one side of straight up the hill, back across it more
often than not but not always, with a stretch of trodden ground at the turn. Each step is a pale
slab of its own laid on the hill, its two ends ragged, as the print's are; now and then a short wall
of squared blocks holds the hill back along one side. Between flights, now and then, the way is a
trail instead: no steps, a gentle rise, bending as it goes, with only a few flat stones and a worn
line of grey strokes to show where it runs, sometimes through an open glade with no trees. (Paths
that branch are to come.)

The hill's height at any point up it is the path's there, so the ground runs up with the steps,
lying a little under them beside the path and rolling further off. The world is made in bands
across the hill, twelve metres deep, as they come into view and dropped as they fall behind, each
from the seed and its number: the ground, the steps and stones that fall in it, standing stones in
little clusters along the path and in outcrops behind the trees, tufts of grass, the trees, fallen
blossom, and the temples. No crown stands over the path, nor just in front of it as the eye looks
up the hill; further down, a crown may stand before it, and the walker passes behind and comes out
again. A tree that would lose most of its blossom to that isn't grown at all.

## The temples

Now and then (never closer than seven pieces apart) a temple. Mostly it stands beside the path, to
the left or right, where a level trail runs through a glade: raised on its stone terrace, railed
round, a stair down its front to the path, and the view leans toward it as you pass. Sometimes, a
third of the time, it's a gate hall across the path instead: a flight climbs straight up to it
between balustrades onto its terrace, the path goes in at its open door, through, and on from
behind. White walls on a grey stone plinth; a porch of red columns on round stone bases; a red beam
with painted panels of teal and blue under it, a row of brackets; a red door, lattice windows; the
plaque with the temple's name in gold on dark wood, hung under the eaves. White walls run off to
either side, capped with tiles.

The roof is hip-and-gable, as the print's is: the lower slopes hipped all round, a red gable at
each end under its barge board; the eaves well out, swept up toward the corners, a row of round
tile-ends along them; every tile drawn, barrel tiles in rows down whichever way each slope falls,
each lapping the next, a gloss along each row's crown; a ridge along the top with its ends curled
in and a tall beaded finial over each, ridges down the gables and hips with little knobs along
them.

## The trees

Each peach tree is grown: a trunk you can see, leaning, kinked, before it opens into three to five
limbs that spread wide and up. Every limb is drawn as the print draws them: straight runs and
angles, not curves, forking sharply, and again, ending in fans of hairline twigs, with twigs out
of the angles along the way. Bark is black with a hard-edged band of cream down the side toward
the light, broken along its length. The blossom gathers in clumps about the twigs' ends and along
the limbs, so a tree is a broad umbrella of pink; the branches are drawn a little nearer the eye
than they are, so they show over the blossom as ink over colour.

## The print

It's drawn as a print, in two steps. First the scene, in its inks: flat colours, hardly lit. With
it, for each pixel, which way its surface faces and what it's made of, and its depth. Then a pass
over the screen prints it, with lines only where the print has them: black ink where things stand
against each other (branches, the hall, the walker); on stone a thinner line, warm grey and broken
as a dry brush leaves it; none about the rocks or the blossom, which stand by their fills alone,
but for a deeper pink where one crown stands before another. Lines are one pixel, on the nearer
side of an edge. Everything that belongs to a thing (where a stone's line breaks, the dabs at a
crown's edge, its washes) is worked out where that thing is on the hill, so it stays put as the
view moves; only the paper's own grain belongs to the sheet. Under it all the paper: its mottle, its fibres, its grain, the print's edge a
shade darker, and the far hill fading into it.

Few inks, flat, as a block prints them. The blossom is one field of pink per crown, not a heap of
balls, in four pinks only: each puff takes its tone from where it is on its tree (lit, high, or
under) and from washes that pool across the whole crown, and where one pink meets the next the
edge is ragged; over that, fine pale flecks. Stone is two: pale tops, one darker face. Shade and
the strokes on the ground are one grey. Its edge crumbles into
the paper in little square dabs, a few loose beyond it, as a worn block prints. Each tree is its
own pink, a little deeper or paler than the next.

Rocks are standing stones, slabs and wedges on end, leaning a little, their faces flat: black
turned from the light, charcoal toward it, grey on top, pocked with small pits. Shade on the
ground is strokes of grey laid across, thinning out at the edges, off to the side away from the
light. Steps are pale slabs with grey strokes along their tops and warmer faces. Fallen blossom
is coral red, the reddest thing in the print: stamped five-lobed flowers in drifts under the
trees, and specks of red between; petals turning as they fall about the walker.

The walker: a grey-blue robe with wide sleeves and a red sash, black hair gathered under a straw
hat, a staff taller than they are, and a stroke of shadow beside them. As they walk, a foot goes
forward and the other back with each step, and the staff swings forward, is planted, and they pass
it.
