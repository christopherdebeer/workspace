# Taohua · 桃花

A journey up a hillside of peach blossom, on forever. There's no one path: ways cross the hill,
parting and meeting, past mountain temples and casts from Changes written on the hillside. After a
print: peach trees in flower, black standing stones, stone steps where the way is steep, a temple
of teal tiles and red columns, a walker in a straw hat with a staff, the paper's cream under it all.

Tap a way and the walker goes there, by whichever ways lead there, up the hill or back down (a
branch that ends goes nowhere else, so to see another, come back to the fork). A tap anywhere else
(the hillside, a temple, a tree, blossom over a way, a cast) is taken down through whatever stands
there to the ground under it, as if it weren't there, and sends them to the nearest place on a way to
that, whether it can be seen or not: a tap on blossom over a way goes to that way, under it, and the
walker may go out of sight under the crowns, as the way does. A tap on open ground that's seen
(the ground itself, not blossom over it; not a rock, a terrace, a cast's sheet, water, or a bank cut
steep) within fifteen metres of a way takes them off the ways, there: they leave the way and pick
their way across the open ground, round whatever's in it, a little more carefully than on a way.
From there, a tap on a way (or the keys) takes them back across the open ground to the nearest way,
and on by the ways. Off the ways, the address keeps where they stand (`?at=@x,z`). The
place a tap found is answered in the scene: a ring of vermilion, left open as a brush draws it, for
a moment. Only a tap walks: a drag, wherever it starts, even on a way, looks about a little; let
go, and the view comes back. With keys, ↑ or W walks on (at a fork, by the way most nearly ahead),
↓ or S back. Pinch (or the wheel) to come nearer in the print, and nearest of all to see through the walker's
eyes: the view lifts to look out along their way while the camera's still well over and behind them,
so they drop out of the bottom of the shot, and only then does it come down to their eyes (blossom
right before the eye thinning away in dabs); there, it looks along their way as they turn, and a
drag looks about; in
the composition view, to stand further off still, up to twice as far. Each view keeps its own. The steps climbed are counted at the top.

The title stands at the foot of the print where the journey starts: the name of the first temple up
the hill from there, over 桃花, large, and a small seal, 桃源. It belongs to the hillside there, not
to the screen, so it moves with the hill and is walked away from, and doesn't come back unless you
walk back down to it.

## The hill and its ways

The hill comes first. It rises up the slope, gently in some bands and steeply in others, the same
all across it.

On it, the ways grow from the foot of the hill like a tree laid on the slope: a branch at a time,
always the lowest first. From where it's reached, each branch goes on (a long reach, 34 to 64
metres, slanting well across the hill, as often back the way it came across as on, so a way
climbs the hill in long zigzags, pulled back toward the middle when it strays far to one side), or
forks in two, the forks heading off to either side; now and then a short spur leaves it, up to a building or a clearing of its own on the
slope; now and then a branch ends, at a building or a clearing. Rarely, two branches that have
drawn near meet again. And where a branch comes near another (16 to 44 metres across the hill)
that it's far from by the ways (more than four times as far, and a hundred metres or more, back
down to where they parted and up again), most often a short path runs across between them,
gently wandering, between plain places on both (never a building's, a gate's or a clearing's, nor
a fork that's already three ways, nor near a terrace), so a way you can see close by is close by
to walk to as well. There are never more than three branches at once, and never fewer than
one: with one, it forks more often; with three, a branch is more likely to end. No way crosses
another, nor runs within eight metres of one, and no two junctions come within sixteen metres;
where a branch has no room to go on, it ends there, in a quiet clearing. A way through a gate
always goes on, by whichever way up the hill it fits; if none fits, the gate was never a gate, and
a hall stands at the end of its branch instead.

No reach is straight: each wanders along its way, a long sway to one side, a turn back, smaller
bends over that, all its own, settling straight only where it comes to a building's stair or
leaves a gate's back. Each way takes the hill's height as it goes: where its going is steep (the hill steep there, or
the way heading straight up it) it climbs in steps, each a pale slab of its own, ragged at its
ends, turning as the way bends, now and then a short wall of squared blocks beside it; where it's
gentle, it's a trail, a few flat stones and a worn line of grey strokes. The ground just downhill
of a way is kept open of trees, so a way running across the hill isn't lost under the crowns in
front of it. All of it from the seed: the same seed, the same hillside.

The world is made in tiles, sixteen metres across and twelve deep, about wherever the walker is,
as far across as the view reaches, as they come into view and dropped as they're left, each from
the seed and its place: the ground, the steps and stones, standing stones along the ways and in
outcrops behind the trees, tufts of grass, the trees, fallen blossom, and the buildings. Crowns
stand as they grow, over a way too: nothing is cut out of a crown to show what's under it.

## The ways, close to

A way meets the hill by degrees, not at a line. Each is benched into the hill: across it and a
little either side, the ground lies level with its middle; uphill of that, a cut bank rises quickly
to the hill, and downhill a fill slope falls more gently to it, the bench's edge and the banks'
widths ragged along the way. From the way's edge out, a fringe of loose pebbles, tufts and
drifted petals thins away into the open hill, in loose groups; by a stair, a few stones spilt from
it lie half sunk in the bank. The worn earth of a trail spreads unevenly either side of it.

Stairs weather: now and then a slab's cracked across, in two; sunk a little and tipped; or broken
at an end, the piece lying beside it. Moss and grass grow in the joint at the back of a tread, where
the next step rises, and fallen petals drift against the risers.

Each stretch is of its own kind. A stair is most often of pale slabs; now and then cut in the black
rock, the outcrop it climbs through standing either side; or earth steps, each held at its front by
a log, pegged at its ends. Before a temple, slabs. A gentle stretch is most often a worn earth trail
with a few flat stones in it; now and then stepping stones through the grass, a stride apart,
alternating; and coming to a temple, flags laid in a cracked-ice pattern (冰裂纹), kerbed along either
edge. Over a marsh, a boardwalk of planks on posts.

At the head of a stair, now and then, there's a landing: a broader place, paved in cracked ice, to
stand and look; by it, a stone lantern (石灯籠), a bench, or a cairn.

The ways are drawn as the water is: the paper, and marks only where something happens, each its own
geometry with its reason (pathmarks.ts), lying on the ground as it's laid. Where a way's cut into the
hill, a line along the top of the cut, where it begins, broken, heavier the higher it stands; and
hanging from it, strokes down the bank, in groups, each leaning its own way, mostly short, now and
then one long, closer where it's steep, fewer where the way turns sharply (strokes square to it there
would fan out). Its bed's edge is a fine line, broken: more of it under a cut than down the hill,
where the way's built out and meets the hill by degrees; none beside a stair or flags, whose slabs
and kerbs are their edge. A trail's trodden earth is a faint warm wash, gone toward its edges, with
now and then a long stroke along it where feet have worn it, crowding the inside of a bend, and where
it's steep, a short wandering runnel. On a stair of slabs, the worn edge of a tread, at the flight's
foot and head and now and then between. All are heavier where something happens (a flight's foot or
head, a way's ends, a crossing) and lighter between. In the marks view (`?marks`) they're coloured by
what made them: edge teal, cut magenta, bank brown, wear blue, tread orange, runnel green.

(seed 1) where a kind of way can be studied, looking up it, with the trees left out: the longest
flight of each kind of stair, the deepest cut beside a trail, its sharpest bend, its steepest stretch.

## The water

Now and then a stream comes down the hill: one every few hundred metres up it, each two to three
hundred metres long. It's laid out before the ways, from the seed alone, a stream at a time up
the hill, so any part of it can be made without the rest. From a spring among rocks it runs down
the hill, wandering across it in long bends and shorter ones, and short wiggles of its own every
few metres, as a stream winds among stones, narrow at its spring and wider
below, to a pond at its foot (or a marsh, its reeds in clumps), where the hill's gentlest
thereabouts. Where the hill's gentle it slows, and now and then pools; where it's steep it runs
white over rocks, or falls: in steps, from pool to pool, each held at its lip by a ledge of rock;
or all at once, from as high as four metres, into a deep pool cut into the hill under a bowl of
cliff.

Its water lies level across it; down it, the level follows the hill, or stands level in a pool
(held at the pool's downhill rim, so the pool's cut into the hill behind rather than banked up
before it), or drops at a fall. The ground's carved to hold it: a bed under it, deeper toward its
middle; banks rising from its edge to meet the hill, steep and rocky by falls and pools cut deep;
raised a little where the hill lies below the water's level, except where another water runs out
through it. Where two waters meet, the ground's carved by both. Its rocks: along its banks, more
where it runs fast, and in it there; ledges at each fall's lip; rocks about each spring and pond.

The ways cross it where they can: once, fairly square to it, where it runs or over a marsh; never
through a pool or at a fall, and no junction, terrace or cast stands by the water. Where both
banks are about the water's level, a crossing is stepping stones (pale, squarish, a stride apart,
just above the water) or a bridge; where the way's steep there, or the water's white, or the banks
are cut high, a bridge: a deck of stone slabs, arched a little, a kerb along either side, a pier at
either bank. No tree stands in the water, on a bank cut steep, nor just downhill of the water,
where its crown would stand between the eye and it: the water's an open mass, as the print's are.

It's drawn as the prints draw water: the paper itself, and ink only where something happens. Its
edge is always there, quietly, since it can't be walked into: a fine line where it meets the hill,
lighter than the ink and broken now and then in long lengths, and just inside it a faint cool wash,
gone half a metre in, as the prints grade water from its bank. Its
marks aren't a texture laid over it but geometry of their own (marks.ts), each with a reason to be
there, read from the water: where a stream bends, its current crowds in short strokes against the
outer bank and draws out long along the inner; where it runs gently, now and then one long low stroke
along it; where it runs fast, short strokes, slanted, crossing; where a stone stands in it, the
current splits about it upstream and trails a wake behind; where it falls, a broken lip across the
top, lines down the sheet of different lengths, and at its foot strokes flung out downstream, the heaviest
marks on the water, and rings spreading, fainter as they go; where it comes into a pool, rings spreading from the inlet; and on still
water, almost nothing: a long line or two across, far apart. Its banks are a broken line, about half
drawn, heavier where they're cut steep or the current's against them; a pond's far shore (up the hill,
where the bank's cut) is drawn, broken, with a few short level strokes just below it on the water where
the bank shows in it, and its near shore left open. Each mark is a ribbon laid on the
water (or down a fall), tapered to a hair at both ends, its width and weight its own, its edge drawn
smooth however thin, broken now and then as a dry brush breaks; it stays where it is as the view moves.
Foam is a stipple at the foot of each drop and in patches where it runs fast; stones in the water have
their rings, and on still water their reflections. Petals float on its stiller water.

cooler.

## The deer

Now and then, not often (in about two stretches of three, each some three hundred metres up the
hill), a few sika deer stand somewhere a little off a way: 梅花鹿, the plum-blossom deer, a warm russet
coat spotted white along the back, a pale belly and a white rump, the stag's antlers branched. One to
three: a stag, a doe, a fawn. Most often, if there's a pool in the stretch, at its edge, drinking;
else out in the open, four to eight metres off a way. No tree stands where they do, nor in front of
them as the eye sees them, so they're seen, as the print sees its figures, in the middle distance.
They're drawn in the print's flat inks and lined in ink like everything else, each made of parts
turned about its joints (the body, the neck, the head, each leg).

They graze (or drink), head down, nibbling, and now and then lift their heads to look about. As the
walker comes within some twenty-four metres they lift their heads and watch, turning a little toward
them; within fourteen, they bound away together (forelegs reaching, hind legs pushing, the body
rising and pitching with each leap), up the hill and off from the ways, turning aside from water,
and stop a good thirty metres off to look back, watching for as long as the walker stays near; left
alone, they graze again. They never come close.

## The temples

Buildings stand where the ways give them a place, each of its kind and from its parts, on a stone
terrace railed round with a stair down its front to the way, facing back down the way it's
reached by and down the hill:

- a **gate hall** across a way now and then: the way climbs its front stair, goes in at its open
  door and out the back, down its back stair, and on; white walls run off to either side;
- at a branch's end, or up a spur: a **hall** (three bays or five; a hip-and-gable roof or a
  hipped one; single eaves or double, with an upper storey; sometimes walls out to its sides), an
  open **pavilion** (columns, benches between, a pyramid roof or a short ridge, its eaves swept
  high), a **pagoda** (three to five tiers, each narrower, each under a skirt of tiles, a tall
  spire), or a small **shrine**.

White walls on a grey stone plinth; red columns on round stone bases; a red beam with painted
panels of teal and blue under it, a row of brackets; a red door, lattice windows; the plaque with
the temple's name in gold on dark wood. Every roof's tiles are drawn, barrel tiles in rows down
whichever way each slope falls, round tile-ends along the eaves, ridges down the hips with knobs
along them, curled ridge ends or a finial at the peak.

## The trees

Each peach tree is grown as the print draws one: a bold trunk you can see, leaning, kinked, before
it opens into three or four limbs that spread up and out to either side across the picture. Every
limb is a long run thinning steadily, wandering a little along its way, then forking two or three
ways at a narrow angle, and again, four times over, ending in a lace of hairline twigs out to the
crown's edge, with spurs out of the turns along the way. The branching spreads across the view,
with little depth toward the eye or away; what depth it has leans a little away, so the near side
of each crown is open and its limbs are seen. Bark is black with a band of cream down the side
toward the light, broken only now and then.

The blossom gathers in clumps about the twigs' ends, each set a little behind its twigs along the
eye's line, and the dome is filled out with more clumps further back, so the crown is one full
mass. The limbs are never drawn through it: they show where the tree's growth and its blossom's
density leave them open, past the crown's edge, on the near side of a crown low down, and through
the thinner crowns (most trees are full, some thinner, each its own).
The blossom's tone keeps
the crown's structure, pale where it's lit and high, deep beneath, dappled with pale dabs and deep
recesses in clusters; each tree is its own pink.

## The print

It's drawn as a print, in two steps. First the scene, in its inks: flat colours, hardly lit. With
it, for each pixel, which way its surface faces and what it's made of, and its depth. Then a pass
over the screen prints it, with lines only where the print has them: black ink where things stand
against each other (branches, the hall, the walker); on stone a thinner line, warm grey and broken
as a dry brush leaves it; none about the rocks or the blossom, which stand by their fills alone,
but for a deeper pink where one crown stands before another. Lines are one pixel, on the nearer
side of an edge. Everything that belongs to a thing (where a stone's line breaks, the dabs at a
crown's edge, its washes) is worked out where that thing is on the hill, so it stays put as the
view moves; only the paper's own grain belongs to the sheet. Under it all the paper: its mottle, its fibres, its grain, the print's edge a
shade darker, and the far hill fading into it.

Few inks, flat, as a block prints them. The blossom is one field of pink per crown, not a heap of
balls, in a narrow range of pink inks: each puff takes its tone from where it is on its tree (lit, high, or
under) and from washes that pool across the whole crown, and where one pink meets the next the
edge is ragged; over that, fine pale flecks. Stone is two: pale tops, one darker face. Shade and
the strokes on the ground are one grey. Its edge crumbles into
the paper in little square dabs, a few loose beyond it, as a worn block prints. Each tree is its
own pink, a little deeper or paler than the next.

Rocks are standing stones, slabs and wedges on end, leaning a little, their faces flat: black
turned from the light, charcoal toward it, grey on top, pocked with small pits. Shade on the
ground is strokes of grey laid across, thinning out at the edges, off to the side away from the
light. Steps are pale slabs with grey strokes along their tops and warmer faces. Fallen blossom
is coral red, the reddest thing in the print: stamped five-lobed flowers in drifts under the
trees, and specks of red between; petals turning as they fall about the walker.

The walker: a grey-blue robe with wide sleeves and a red sash, black hair gathered under a straw
hat, a staff taller than they are, and a stroke of shadow beside them. As they walk, a foot goes
forward and the other back with each step, the staff swings forward, is planted, and they pass it;
the body sways from foot to foot and twists a little with each step, leans into a climb, the free
arm swings against the stride, and the pace surges and eases within each step. They turn to face
their way gradually. Standing, they breathe, and look about, now one way, now another.

Broad, asymmetric openings beside the route alternate with denser wooded shoulders, each opening
held by one tree standing alone toward its far side. These composition fields span streaming
bands; jittered trees belong to the band containing their final position. Trees vary in stature.
The broad blossom wash is fixed to cluster centres; fine marks remain attached to the illustrated
lobes. Distant paper fade starts beyond the usual focal distance.

## The journey's movements

The way up is told in movements, as a scroll is: every stretch of about 170 metres along the
climb has its own character, from the seed and the stretch's number, and the hill eases from one
to the next over the last thirty metres of each. An orchard is the hill as it has always been; a
grove draws the trees close and deep; an open stretch lets the paper through, a tree here and
there; a lone stretch is nearly empty, a single tree or two standing out at its openings; a
stretch of stones thins the trees and sets rocks about the hillside. The first stretch is always
orchard, so every journey begins among the blossom.

A tree that stands alone is drawn as a print draws one it means you to look at: a little taller,
with more twigs at the ends of its limbs, and its blossom held back so the branching shows
through. Trees in a grove stay as they were, one full mass among many.

## Changes along the way

Before any tree is grown, the ways are given their places for Changes, each a space kept open on
the paper, as the prints keep paper clear for their verse: where a branch ends in a clearing,
there, just past its end; at some forks, in the space up the hill between the forks; and beside
about a quarter of the longer ways, on whichever side has room. Each is clear of every way and
well apart from the others. Each holds a cast from Changes, from the seed and the station's
number: six lines, each as three coins fall, so old lines come as often as they would. The cast is
written in its clearing, as a print sets its verse in the paper it keeps open: the
hexagram's six lines, its moving lines vermilion; its name in a column beside them, and, if it
moves, 之 and the name of what it becomes; under them, small, the numbers and names in English.
The words stand on a sheet at the uphill side of the clearing, always turned to face the eye (the
drawing turns each corner of it about its foot), so they read square however the view moves; but
they're of the hill, so whatever stands before them (a crown, a rock) stands before them, and nothing
has to make room for them. They come from a sheet of words, ink on nothing: where there's no ink,
the hill shows through. They only
read (a tap on them walks, like a tap on the hillside). Beside it, a small built thing ties it to the place: a cairn of
flat stones, each smaller than the last (always where Mountain or Earth is in the cast), or a
wayside shrine, a stone post with a little white niche under a roof of teal tiles.

The cast shapes its space too. Its upper trigram is what stands behind it: Mountain, standing
stones along its upper edge; Wind, a grove close behind; Thunder, one tree well out at its far
side, framing it. Its lower trigram is what lies in it: Water, stepping stones across it; Lake, a ring
of flat stones; Fire, fallen blossom heaped red; Mountain, one low stone; Wind, grass in tufts;
Thunder, a stone split in two. Heaven and Earth leave it open. Trees stand well back from a station's
edge (a crown spreads four metres and more, so its trunk stands that much further off), further on
its downhill side, where a crown would stand over the space as the eye sees it; every tile near a
station keeps it open, not only the one it stands in.
