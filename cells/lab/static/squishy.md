# Squishy

A third-person platformer in which you are a dumpling: a steamed one, of dough, with a face;
from level 13 (and, once you've been there, by the word at the bottom, anywhere) a clear pink
jelly squishy toy of one, full of glitter, with a pointed tip. You don't walk: you flick. Take
hold of the dumpling itself, drag back and let go, as with a slingshot, and it flies the other
way, as hard as you pulled. Touch anywhere else and you're moving the camera. Through a kitchen, place by place, to the golden steamer: home.
First the preparation table, a place laid out as it is (a spoon to land in, a bench with a towel
thrown over it, butter melted across the wood); then the counter by the window (a loaf, a
buttered tray, a bread bin, a shelf you climb up to by its towel); then the sink (a sponge, soap
bubbles, the drainer, the dish rack, and over the water to the sill); then the stovetop, and
the whole kitchen.

It opens on its title: its name in soft dough letters over the first place, the dumpling sat
on its cloth and the jelly beside it (still to come, a treat for later), both turned to you. They're
there to play with: take hold of either and pull, and it stretches after your finger, as far as
it'll go, and springs back when you let go; tap one and it dents and wobbles. They don't go
anywhere: the title is where to feel how each of them gives. Left alone a while, now and then one
of them hops. New Game starts at the beginning; Continue, once you've got
somewhere, where you left off; Settings has the sound, which dumpling (once the jelly's come),
and any level you've reached to start at (a level's open once the one before it's been got home;
the jelly's yours once level 12 has been). `?lab` opens everything, with a level back and on, and the
dumpling either way, at the foot of the glass. In play, the word `menu` at the bottom comes back here. (A link to a
level, `?level=3`, goes straight to it.)

Where you take hold of it is where it's held: the dough under your finger is drawn out toward it as
you pull, stretching, the rest of it hanging back, and it winds up, squeezed along the way it's
going with its eyes shut tight. (It's picked by its skin as you see it: the nearest bit of it under
your finger, the side facing you, with a little room round it for a fingertip.) A dotted arc shows where its middle will go, ending in a ring where it lands: green
for a top further on, cream for one already reached, red for nowhere (the floor). The band on
the glass shows how hard. Its middle flies a true parabola, so the arc is exact up to where it
touches something. You can only flick from something, but a flick let go in the air waits a
moment (a third of a second) and goes as it touches.

A finger lifted still flicks just as aimed. A finger *swiped* as it lets go gives spin, and the
aim is taken from before the swipe, so the swipe doesn't change where it goes. Swiped on, the
way it will fly, it has topspin: it rolls on when it lands, two or three times as far, which is
how you cross a pan or reach the far side of a board. Swiped back, it has backspin: it bites
where it lands and stays, even on butter. A word at the bottom says which you gave it.

A tap on it is a poke: it dents where you touched it, the way your finger went into the glass,
and rolls away from it, a hand's width and no more, with hardly a hop. Poked at its back, it rolls
on; poked from above, it only squashes and wobbles. For creeping to an edge, off a lid, or back
to the middle of a top.

Dragging off the dumpling turns the camera round it and tilts it, and it stays where you put it
until your next flick, when it goes back to following.

## Stuck to a wall

Stuck to something's side (honey's splat, or a towel's grip) it isn't held up stiff: it hangs.
Held at the patch that's stuck, the rest of it goes limp and droops, slumping down off the patch,
the dough longer (half as tall again as it stands) and the jelly less so; let go, it gathers itself
back. From there you can jump off, aiming away from the wall, or climb: aim into the wall, and it
pushes up the wall, sliding, hardly gripping, and once its middle clears the top, over and onto
it (a mantle). The arc shows that too, up the wall and over to where it'll land, so how hard is
yours to judge: too soft and it slides back down to where it was; too hard and it goes over and
off the far side.

Fall and it's a splat, and back to the last spot you stood on. There's no safe ground anywhere:
every place is made of stations, things standing at their own sizes with a spot or two up on each,
and the table's or the counter's top beneath them is the fall, as the sink's water is. The clock runs
until you're home, the count of falls beside it, and your best time for each level is kept. Each
level opens with its place's name.

Levels 1 and 2 are The Preparation Table; 3 and 4, The Counter by the Window; 5 and 6, The
Sink (each below). Level 7 moves to the stovetop, a new kitchen with its own things (below); from
11 on, the whole kitchen, mixed.

## The Preparation Table

A wooden table, laid out by hand and varied a little each time, its top the fall. You start on a
folded cloth on a stack of cookbooks. A ramekin, and a wooden spoon laid from it up to a painted tin
bench: land in the spoon's bowl (it catches you) and go up its handle. The bench, its far end under a
tea towel thrown over it and hanging down both sides: linen grips, and a throw at the towel's hanging
side clings a moment or two before it lets go. Then the butter dish up on its tin (its butter slips,
and stays on you), the floury paddle board laid across two jars (flour gives a buttered dumpling its
grip back), a tea tin, and home. The second time the gaps are wider, the bench is taller, and a plate
is tipped up on a teacup, up on a cake tin, on the way: a tilted top, glazed, a little slippery.

The spoon, the towel and the tipped plate are sheets: surfaces made of triangles, with a thickness
under them, met exactly as they're drawn. The table, the bench, the board and the tins are the solid
shapes everything else is made of.

## The Counter by the Window

A butcher-block counter against a tiled wall, a window in it, the counter the fall. From a folded
cloth on a biscuit tin: a round loaf in a woven bread basket on two flour canisters, the loaf soft
under its crust (a jelly, a dead one: it gives, and doesn't throw you) and crumby, the basket floured
inside, so where you land decides what you pick up, and its rim catches a bounce; a baking tray on a
cooling rack, butter melted across one corner of it, a lip all round to stop a slide; a tall cake stand,
its plate sticky with hundreds and thousands; the bread bin, deep, out from the wall. Above it, out of
reach of any flick (a flick goes off at the one angle, so from below a high shelf by a wall, the arc
meets the wall first), a shelf, a tea towel hung over its front edge: flick at the towel, it holds
you, and climb up it and over onto the shelf. Fall off the towel and you're back on the bread bin, to
try again. Home is the golden steamer on the window sill. A knife block and a crock of wooden spoons
stand about. The second time, a konjac jelly on a tin to bounce on comes before the cake stand, and the
shelf is higher.

Every hop is checked as it's laid, the towel's too: a flick from the bread bin that lands against the
towel, low enough to hold and high enough that a climb from there gets up and over.

## The Sink

The counter round a steel sink, the window over it; the counter's the fall, and so's the water. From a
folded dishcloth on an upturned pudding basin: a sponge up on a tin (soft, a dead jelly: it soaks up a
landing), its green scourer up; a soap dish up on a tin, heaped round its rim with bubbles, which bounce
you and hold nothing; a two-tier dish rack, its steel drip tray grooved and sloping down toward the
sink, wet, so it slips, and above it the wire rack, its rails to land across, two plates stood up at its
far end. Then across the sink, at the dishcloth hung over the window sill's edge: it holds you, and you
climb it onto the sill. Home along the sill. The second time the rack and the sill are higher and the
dishcloth shorter.

From a tea towel folded on the toaster, two ways: down to a roasting tray on a cooling rack, buttered

Every way is made sure of (`hop`, from each spot to each it leads to), and played through: both

(tap it to fold it). A place says, for each spot, which to make for next (for the camera's look ahead
and for playing itself), and the way the opening view goes over it.

## The kitchen, from the stovetop on

wall with windows in it, made of pieces, each laid where the last left off: one thing at the

## What things stand on

A kitchen's things aren't stood on towers of tins. Each top stands on something with a shape of its
own, from a small vocabulary (supports.ts), each built from what it stands on up to its seat:

- **a stool**: three legs splayed out under a round seat, rungs between them a third of the way up;
- **a little bench**: a square seat on four legs, rails along two sides;
- **a cake stand**: a round foot, a neck flaring out of it, a thin stem, a broad plate;
- **a trestle**: a board laid across two jars, or tea tins, or mugs upturned;
- **a crate**, turned over: slats round its sides with dark between, lid boards across the top;
- **cookbooks**, stacked a little anyhow, each its own size, turned and set off a little;
- **a bowl upturned**: its foot ring the seat, its shoulders falling away round it to its rim;
- **a cooling rack** on two tins: wires close together, a frame round them, little feet;
- and tea tins and jars under a board, as before.

What's met is kept simple and what's drawn is what it is; but where it would be played with, what's
met is what's drawn: a stool's legs and rungs, a cake stand's thin neck, a rack's wires, an upturned
bowl's shoulders (a sheet, so you slide off them). A support too short of itself for where it's
wanted is stood on another: a bowl on a crate, a rack on cookbooks. Any top can go on any support that
can hold it (a steamer on a stool or a rack, a pudding on a cake stand, the butter dish on a crate, a
pan on a board across jars), and now and then it's set off to one side of a broader seat, the rest of
the seat a lower ledge. The room has its say: on the stove, crates, racks, boards across jars, tins,
little stools; past it, anything. `?bench=supports` is one of each, in a run over a table.

## The stovetop

From level 7 the course is on the hob: brushed steel, burners under the steamers, the tiles behind.
A pot, a pan or a jar is the size it is, and where it's wanted higher it stands on a stand: a
wooden board on a stack of tea tins, now and then a celadon jar among them.

- **A frying pan** on a pot: wide, a little slippery (seasoned iron), with a rim round it that
  catches a slide, and its handle out to one side.
- **A butter dish**: the butter is slippery, so a landing slides, a long way at speed; the dish
  has a lip all round that stops you. Backspin bites instead. From against the lip, a flick over
  it catches on it (the arc shows the catch), so flick away from it, or hop to the middle first.
- **A honey jar**, from level 8: a big one, high on its stand, its top higher than a flick reaches, with honey run down its side. You
  don't land on it: you land *against* it, splat into the honey, and stick, hanging there,
  until you flick on. The course turns sharply at a jar. Honey holds whatever touches it, so a
  flick into the jar from the honey climbs it a little and sticks again, higher.
- **A pot with its lid on**, from level 9: the lid's shut, but every few seconds the steam in the
  pot rattles it, six quick jumps of a centimetre or so on the rim, steam escaping round it, and
  it settles again. On the lid, you're jolted; flicked in the middle of a rattle, you go
  unsteadily. The arc knows the rattle.

Every level is made from its number, so level 8 is always the same level, and every hop in it
is checked when it's made: from the middle of each top there is a flick that lands on the next;
onto a lid, at some moment of its cycle; from a honey jar, from a little lower on it too, and on
forward, away from the jar; up to a shelf, a flick against its towel and a climb from there. Playing
itself, it waits out a lid's rattle before it flicks from it or onto it.

## Jellies

A jelly gives, and what it does with a landing is set by what it should do, not left to come out of
how it wobbles. The dumpling sinks into a jelly's top as into a bed: a damped spring under the whole
of it that firms up the further in it goes (k x (1 + (x/x0)²) − c v), measured on its middle from
where its middle was as its foot touched (so its own squash, or tack drawing its foot down, can't
pump it). Each jelly is three numbers for that: how much of a landing it gives back (its
restitution), how far into it (of its height) a gentle landing goes (from 10 cm), and how far a hard
one (from 50 cm). From those, the spring and how it firms; and it's damped as stiff as it is where
it is, so it gives back as much of a hard landing as of a gentle one. Of a gentle landing (from
under about 20 cm) it gives back less, so a bouncing dumpling goes boing, boing, and settles. It
holds the dumpling from sliding as hard as it pushes, so a landing sticks where it lands, and a
bounce goes straight up. A flick off a jelly goes as aimed: it lets go, and doesn't throw it harder
for what it had sunk in.

Its top is a membrane: nodes on rings and spokes out from its middle, each held to its height by a
spring, damped, and drawn toward its neighbours. While the dumpling's in it, the top is held to the
dumpling's foot, flat under it and falling away round it; when it leaves, it's let go with some of
the speed it had, and wobbles on and ripples.

Each is lit as a jelly is: glowing through, brightest where it's thinnest as seen (its edges), the
mango pudding flecked with bits of fruit under its caramel top, the konjac seen partly through, a few
small bubbles held in it. Three kinds, by how they give: a **panna cotta** (cream, under a berry
coulis) is soft and keeps a landing (it gives back a fifth of it, or nothing of a gentle one); a
**mango pudding** gives most of it back (about three quarters: from 25 cm, up 13); a **konjac jelly**
(clear pink) is firm and gives nearly all of it back (about nine tenths: from 25 cm, up 21; from 40,
up 33), dented a centimetre and a half by a gentle landing and three by a hard one. A loaf and a
sponge are soft and dead. The aiming line, landing on a jelly, goes on up in fainter dots as high as
it'll be thrown.

`?bench=jelly` is the jelly bench: the three of them in a row on a table, between the cloth and
home, and a panel to try them: pick one, change how much it gives back, how far in a gentle and a
hard landing go, and how its top wobbles, as you watch, and drop the dumpling on it from so high,
again and again. `node cells/lab/devtools/squishy-drops.mjs` measures them: each dumpling, with and
without sprinkles, dropped on each from 10, 25 and 50 cm: how long it's in, how fast it comes in and
leaves, how high it comes back up and so its restitution, how far in it goes.

## What gets on it

Some tops have something on them that comes off on the dumpling where it touches, and stays
there, on that part of it, wearing off in time and shaken off by a hard landing; while it's on,
it has its say in how it lands:

- **Flour** (on every chopping board): dry. It grips better, and loses its own tack, so it
  won't hold to a wall.
- **Butter** (the butter dish): slippery. It slides far on anything, can't hold a wall, and
  honey won't hold a buttered patch: a buttered dumpling thrown at a jar slides off it.
- **Crumbs** (two plates in five): rough. It bounces where it lands, and takes longer to settle.
- **Pepper** (one plate in four): it sneezes, a little while after, a hop any way, and the pepper's
  off it. On a small top, mind.
- **Hundreds and thousands** (most puddings): sweet and sticky. More tack: it holds walls
  better.

It's picked up where it touches, and a hard landing into it splashes it up its sides, as far up
as it came down hard: set down gently, only its bottom is floured; dropped from a height, most of
it. You can see all of it: the flour's dusting, whiter and chalkier than the dough, in clumps, thicker in the creases, butter's yellow sheen,
crumbs stuck on in lumps, pepper's specks, the sprinkles' coloured rods, each on the part that
touched, riding its wobble. The first of each is named at the bottom when you pick it up.

## The dumpling

It is soft for real: a skin of particles in its shape (562 for the jelly's gumdrop, broad on a
flat bottom and drawn up into its tip; 450 for the dough's xiaolongbao, twelve pleats twisting
up to a knot), held to that shape by shape matching (Müller et al. 2005: each step,
the rotation that best fits where the particles are, from Müller, Bender et al.'s 2016
iteration, and the particles drawn back toward the rest shape so turned, partly allowed to
stretch). It isn't fitted only as a whole: twelve overlapping patches of it are each fitted on their own as
well, and each particle drawn toward a blend of the whole's fit and its patches', so a part of it
can bend, droop and dent while the rest keeps its shape. Hanging, it goes limp (its pull back to
shape eased, more of it left to the patches), which is the droop. Its volume is kept as a constraint of its own, so a hard landing squashes it wide
and flat rather than smaller, and it bulges at the sides. It's stepped ten times a frame
(position-based dynamics: move, fit, keep the volume, push out of what it touches, and take
the velocities from where everything went).

The two are made differently: the jelly springs back faster and keeps more of each wobble, so a
landing squashes it to under half its height and it jiggles back; the dough is slower and
deader, and plops.

Where it touches, there's friction (Coulomb, from how hard it was pushed out; it holds where it
touches, so it sits on a slope as long as the slope's grip will hold it, on a ramp of 20° or a
tipped plate, and slides off a steep one), bounce where the surface has any, and tack: dough is sticky, and the jelly nearly as much, so it holds to what it
touches, a wall included for a moment, and the tack tires on a wall so it slides down. A towel's
grip lasts longer and tires too, so a throw at the towel hangs a moment or two before it lets go.
As it's flicked off something, what it's leaving hardly grips it, for a moment, so an edge can't
snag it and tip it over as it goes. Butter
has no tack to give. Honey has more than the dumpling's own, and it never tires: a particle
that touches honey is held where it touched, the whole body is slowed hard so its momentum
doesn't tear it off the patch that's stuck, and each step the rest of it is drawn back up so
that it hangs from the patch rather than sagging off it.

Spin is what you gave it at the flick, spent as it lands: with topspin it really rolls, the
roll is let live and the grip eased for a moment; with backspin the grip is doubled, the tack
quick to hold and the roll killed, so it plants. Its wobble is damped against its
moving as one body (its middle's motion and its turning), so it jiggles and settles rather
than ringing. On the ground its roll dies and it rights itself like a roly-poly: tipped over, on its side or
its head, it rolls back over the edge it's lying on onto its flat bottom, its bottom flat to
whatever it's on (a slope's up, not the world's: righted to the world's on a slope, it would
crawl down it); a small lean it leaves alone. Sat still, it shuffles round to look at you.

The face is drawn on its rest shape, so it turns, squashes and stretches with it: round eyes
with a shine that blink, red cheeks, squeeze shut `> <` while you wind up, an `o` in the air, a happy `^ ^` at home.

## Drawing

WebGL2. The light is a window's: a soft shadow map (the shadow sharper near what casts it),
the course in its materials (woven bamboo with its rims, glazed porcelain, a pudding with a
caramel top, lacquer, painted tins, a chopping board's grain, chopsticks with lacquered ends),
the tiled wall with its windows behind (subway tiles in a brick bond, a warm white glaze, pale grout, the room's light bounced up onto them), steam in the air, in wisps that thin out at their edges and never come close enough to blur the glass.

The jelly is seen through. What's behind it is copied and drawn again through it, bent as a lens
of jelly bends it (refracted, from how far through it the eye looks at each point) and tinted
the deeper it goes (red comes through; green and blue are taken), with a little of the light
caught inside it, pinker at its thin rim and glowing where the window is behind it. In it, a
thousand flakes of glitter, each carried by the four nearest particles of the skin so they
squash and wobble with it, each a tiny mirror turned its own way: one flashes when it catches
the window, as the jelly turns or jiggles. Over it all its gloss (the room in it, the window
sharp) and the printed face. Where the window's light comes through it to the ground, a pink
light gathers in its shadow.

The dough is lit as dough:
thick, the light wrapping round it and glowing a little through its thin edges, darker in its
pleats, faintly floury, with a dark under it on whatever it's over.

A level, the first time it's played, opens on the way over it: from high over home, back along
the course top by top, coming down to where the dumpling sits at the start, so you've seen where
you're going before you go (a touch, or a key, cuts it short; started over, it doesn't come again;

The camera follows easily, but if the dumpling nears the edge of the glass, or leaves it, the
camera catches up at once. Winding up, it holds still under the finger, standing back only (slowly)

Sat still on a top, it stands back and looks a little toward the one that comes after that top (the top it's on, not the last it sat on: gone on along the ground past some, it isn't turned back to them; on the ground, or hanging on something, it doesn't look ahead, it only follows),
far enough off to have both in view, so you can judge the flick; once it's flown, it comes back in.
