# A brief without a picture

This describes a game by what happens in it and how it feels to play, and nothing else. It says nothing about what anything looks like, what it is made of, where it is set, what it sounds like, or what it resembles. That is deliberate: it is written for someone who will decide all of that, and who should not have to un-see an existing answer first. There is a working version; please don't look at it before you have ideas of your own.

Every noun below is a placeholder chosen to be as empty as possible. "The object" and "targets" need not be solid things, or things at all. Rename all of it.

## The situation in one paragraph

You send an object away from you. Something pulls it back. On its way out it meets targets and clears them; then it returns, and you have to meet it with your hand at the place it arrives, and send it away again. How your hand meets it decides where it goes next and how hard. Clear enough of what is in front of you and you advance to what lies beyond. Fail to meet the returning object five times and it's over. It never ends otherwise; you are measured by how far you got.

## The three things that exist

**The surface.** The player's whole touch screen is one boundary of the play space: the boundary nearest the player. Nothing else is an input. There is no avatar, tool or cursor that the player steers; the hand acts where it touches. One rule must survive any presentation: *a touch lands on the place it appears to land.* If the object is about to arrive at a point, the player touches that point.

**The object.** There is one. It moves in the space beyond the surface, which has an *away* direction (out from the surface) and two *across* directions (the two directions of the surface itself). Across, the space is bounded, and its bounds are the edges of the surface: the object rebounds from them and never leaves. Away, the space has no end.

**The targets.** They sit at intervals along the away direction, in groups. Each group spreads across the space and leaves gaps. Groups differ from one another in arrangement, and get denser the further out they are. Most targets clear on one contact; further out, some take two.

## How the object moves

This is the part that makes the game feel the way it does. It behaves like a light, lively, real thing that has been thrown, not like a token moving at a set speed.

- **It is pulled back toward the surface, always.** Sent away, it slows continuously. If it meets nothing it stops, hangs for a moment, and returns, arriving about as fast as it left. Nothing else brings it back. There is no pull in the across directions.
- **How hard it is sent is how far out it gets.** A gentle send does not reach the nearest group at all. A hard one reaches the group after next.
- **It only has the energy the hand gave it.** A rebound, from a bound of the space or from a target, keeps most of its speed (about nine tenths) and not all. A rally is exactly as fast as the player makes it.
- **Sent hard enough, it goes through.** Meeting an ordinary target squarely and fast, it clears it and carries on outward, a fifth slower, possibly into the next one, or the next group. Otherwise it clears the target and rebounds. A two-contact target always turns it.
- **It can get behind things.** Having passed through or between targets, it comes back onto the far side of the group and clears its way home through it.
- **It carries the hand's sideways motion as a lingering bend.** If the hand was moving across as it sent the object, the object's path curves that way for about a second, or until a rebound takes most of the curve off.

The consequence for rhythm: the closer the player is to the nearest group, the shorter and sharper each exchange. Far away it is slow, lofted and deliberate. Close up it is rapid.

## What the hand is

A finger on the surface is a hand. Several fingers are several hands. What a hand does to the object is decided at the instant the object arrives under it, by two facts about the finger: is it moving, and how long has it been down.

| as the object arrives, the finger is | the hand is | and the object |
|---|---|---|
| still, and only just down | a **catch** | stops and stays under the finger for as long as it is held, going where the finger goes; it is sent when the finger lifts, in the direction and with the force the finger has at that moment |
| still, and long down | a **rest** | springs straight back out, deflected by where on the hand it landed (centre: straight; edge: sharply away from centre), about as hard as it arrived and never feebly |
| moving, and only just down | a **strike** | leaves in the finger's direction, as hard as the finger was moving; the greatest change of direction available |
| moving, and long down | a **carried rest** | leaves partly in the finger's direction and partly by where it landed; well under half the turn of a strike |

Between "only just down" and "long down" the strike fades into the carried rest smoothly, over about half a second.

What this gives the player, without any modes or buttons:

- A finger left down keeps the object going by itself, safely and a little bluntly.
- Sliding that finger to get under the object turns the return into something with intent.
- Lifting off and touching down again at the last moment is a catch. It is the only one that needs timing, and it buys what the others can't: a pause, a look, a wind-up, a chosen throw.
- Force is always the finger's own speed. A flick is a hard, flat send. Simply lifting a still finger is a soft toss straight out, enough to reach what is nearest and no more.

A hand has a reach: the object need only arrive near the finger, not under its exact centre. A moving hand reaches a little further than a still one. The object can still be met a moment after it has passed the surface.

The very first object, and each replacement after a failure, waits at the surface until the player picks it up and sends it.

## Advancing

- The player's surface moves outward on its own whenever the way ahead is clear, quickly, and comes to rest a short, fixed distance before the nearest group that still has targets. The player never steers this.
- A group does not have to be cleared completely. Once most of it is gone (three fifths, or ten targets, whichever is fewer), the remainder gives way all at once and the surface moves on to the next.
- The object is never left behind by this: if the surface catches up with an object travelling outward, it is carried ahead of it.
- Further out, everything is somewhat quicker: the surface travels faster between groups and the hand sends the object a little harder.

## Failing

If the object returns through the surface and no hand is there, one of five chances is gone and a new object waits to be picked up. After the fifth, the run ends. The score is distance reached. There is no time limit and nothing else that can hurt the player.

## What the player must be able to perceive

Whatever form the game takes, play depends on the player knowing these things, at these moments. How each is conveyed (to the eye, the ear, the hand) is open.

1. **Where the returning object will arrive on the surface, and when,** early enough to move a finger there: about a second's notice. This is the single most important thing, and the hardest, because the object is travelling straight at the player.
2. **How far out the object is right now,** and whether it is going or coming.
3. **Where the object is when something is between it and the player.** It must never be simply lost.
4. **What the hand would be at this instant** (catch, rest, strike), since the same finger can be any of them a third of a second apart.
5. **How hard a send was,** as it happens, and whether it went through or rebounded.
6. **Which targets remain in the nearest group, where its gaps are, and how close it is to giving way.**
7. **What lies beyond the nearest group,** enough to make a hard throw a decision.
8. **That the surface is advancing, has stopped, or has just broken through.**
9. **How many chances are left, and how far the player has come.**

## What it should feel like

- **Physical.** The player is throwing and catching something with weight and spring, with their own hand, and the hand's speed is felt in the result.
- **Fast when close, patient when far.** The tempo comes from distance, not from a clock.
- **Generous to hold, rewarding to time.** Leaving a finger down is always enough to survive. Everything better than surviving comes from moving and from timing.
- **Committed.** A hard throw is a risk: it comes back hard.
- **Unbroken.** No levels, no screens between, no stopping; one continuous outward travel punctuated by breaking through.

## What is deliberately not decided here

Everything about appearance and setting. What the object, the targets, the bounds and the surface are, or whether they are things at all. Whether the away direction reads as forward, upward, downward, inward, into the past, or as no direction. Whether the pull reads as weight or as something else. The shape of the play space and of anything in it. Light, colour, material, texture, line, typography. Sound and music. Whether there is a world around any of this. What clearing a target is. What advancing is. What failing is. The name.

Also open, though it touches mechanics: how the targets within a group are arranged (currently a regular lattice of equal cells; nothing depends on that), and how the nine things above are conveyed.
